<div id="about"></div>

I am a PhD candidate in economics at the Central European University (Vienna), expecting to complete my thesis in June 2027. My research focuses on international trade, industrial policy, applied microeconomics, and economic history.

I am especially interested in evaluating 19th-century industrial policies using natural experiments. Two of my projects study export promotion in Austria-Hungary: the information provided by buyer directories, and participation in international trade fairs. I am also a doctoral researcher on the project “Globalization and Industrial Policy: Evidence from the Austro-Hungarian Monarchy (IP-KuK)” with Gábor Békés and Claudia Steinwender.

<h2 id="research" class="home-section">Research</h2>

<h3 class="research-subsection">Working Papers</h3>

<div class="paper">
  <p class="paper-title">Finding Buyers: Information and Trade Promotion in the First Globalization <em style="font-weight: normal;">(JMP)</em> <a class="paper-btn" href="/files/JMP.pdf">PDF</a></p>
  <details class="paper-abstract" open>
    <summary>Abstract</summary>
    <p>Information frictions impede the formation of buyer–supplier linkages and are particularly severe across national borders. Because information about foreign buyers is costly to acquire privately but can be shared at negligible marginal cost, governments fund export promotion agencies to supply it. This paper estimates how much providing publicly available information about foreign buyers increases firms’ export market participation. Identification comes from the administratively predetermined rotation of buyer listings published by 19th-century Austro-Hungarian consular offices. They covered only a few countries per issue for reasons of space constraints rather than commercial priority. The setting isolates the information channel since consular listings were close to the only public signal firms received about buyers abroad. I construct a novel panel linking 5,500 Austro-Hungarian exporting firms over 1890–1898 to decompose which features of public buyer information drive exports. Publication alone raises the probability of entering a destination by 5.7% over the baseline of 4.9%. When the listed buyers are predominantly intermediaries, the effect reaches 18.3% and entrants are more likely to survive past the first year. Buyer counts matter as much as buyer identity for entering new markets.</p>
  </details>
</div>

<div class="paper">
  <p class="paper-title">What Happens in Paris, Does Not Stay in Paris: Trade Fairs and Search and Matching Frictions <em style="font-weight: normal;">(Submitted)</em> <a class="paper-btn" href="/files/paris_exhibition.pdf">PDF</a></p>
  <p class="paper-authors">with <a href="https://gaborbekes.com/">Gábor Békés</a> and <a href="https://sites.google.com/site/clsteinwender/">Claudia Steinwender</a></p>
  <p class="paper-links">
    <span class="paper-tag">CEPR Discussion Paper 21574 · CESifo Working Paper No. 12714 · CEP Discussion Paper No. 2192</span>
  </p>
  <details class="paper-abstract">
    <summary>Abstract</summary>
    <p>Search and matching frictions prevent firms from forming international trade linkages. Despite trade fairs being a common and often subsidized tool to overcome these frictions, we lack causal evidence on how they facilitate link formation. We exploit a unique feature of Hungarian firms' participation in the 1900 Paris World Exhibition, where a trial exhibition revealed firms’ ex-ante export potential category to develop a novel bounding strategy that compares treated firms to control groups from “above” and “below” in export potential. To implement our empirical strategy, we constructed a novel panel dataset of approximately 3,600 Hungarian manufacturing firms for the 1896–1906 period by digitizing, parsing and linking over 12,000 records across eleven historical sources, including exhibition catalogs, government surveys, commercial directories, official gazettes and patenting directories. We find that participation increases export probability by 4-10 percentage points, patenting probability by 4-6 percentage points and employment by 16–23% over eight years. Effects are larger when firms face fewer domestic competitors and more potential international buyers. This highlights both matching benefits and congestion effects when search and matching frictions are reduced.</p>
  </details>
</div>

<h3 class="research-subsection">Work in Progress</h3>

<div class="paper">
  <p class="paper-title">Foreign Occupation and National Irredentism: Evidence from Hungary</p>
  <p class="paper-authors">with <a href="https://sites.google.com/site/piquericardo/home">Ricardo Pique</a></p>
  <details class="paper-abstract">
    <summary>Abstract</summary>
    <p>We examine the impact of military occupation by a rival neighbor on support for nationalist and irredentist movements. Following WWI, Hungary was invaded by Romanian forces. While much of the country came under Romanian control, the region east of the Tisza River endured a longer and more intense occupation. Using a spatial regression discontinuity design and settlement-level data, we first show that the eastern-bank settlements experienced higher rates of victimization during the occupation. Afterwards, these areas showed greater support for nationalist parties. As the far-right rose to prominence in the advent of WWII, these settlements were more likely to back extremist right-wing movements that prioritized Hungarian irredentist claims over the ruling traditional nationalist party. These findings highlight a strong connection between military occupation and support for extreme nationalist movements.</p>
  </details>
</div>
